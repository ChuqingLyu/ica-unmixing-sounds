Unmixing the Class Band

A small ICA demo for music class: pulling instruments back out of mixed recordings

View source on GitHub

About this project

I made this for music class. Kids already practice picking out instruments by ear when they listen to a song. Here they can watch a computer try to do the same thing.

It is part of my research on helping kids understand AI by trying it with their own hands, inside subjects they already take. You change the mix, listen, and compare. No formulas needed.

Where the idea came from

I wanted kids to meet ICA somewhere familiar and fun (their music class) and to see what it can do in real life. The point isn't the math behind ICA. It's to make the cocktail party problem something they can actually hear and see being solved.

The cocktail party problem: at a noisy party, you can still follow one friend's voice while everyone else is talking. Can a computer do that too?

What is ICA?

ICA stands for Independent Component Analysis. When several instruments play at once, each microphone records a blend of all of them. ICA is a way for a computer to find the separate sounds hidden inside those blends, using only the recordings. It works because each instrument does its own thing, so the computer looks for tracks that don't depend on each other. It's a bit like picking out the piano in a song by ear.

The story

Your class band plays a song together. Three microphones around the music room each pick up all the instruments at once. Can we get each instrument's own track back, using only the microphone recordings?

1 · The band (each instrument alone)

This is what each instrument sounds like by itself. The song is “Twinkle Twinkle Little Star.”

2 · Microphones in the music room

Each mic is in a different spot, so it hears the instruments at different loudness. The numbers say how loud each instrument is at each mic (0 = can't hear it, 1 = very loud).

3 · Pull the instruments apart (ICA)

The computer only gets the three mic recordings. It never hears the instruments alone.

Heads up: the computer doesn't know which instrument is which or how loud it was, so it may give them back in a different order, at a different volume, or even upside down (which sounds the same). Under each track we show which instrument it sounds most like.

How it works

Each mic recording is a mix: a bit of piano, plus a bit of drums, plus a bit of bass, with different amounts at each mic. ICA tries to undo the mix by finding tracks that are as independent as possible, meaning knowing one track tells you nothing about the others. A simpler method called PCA only looks for the loudest, most spread-out patterns, and that isn't enough to pull instruments apart. The version used here, FastICA, works in steps. First it centers and balances the recordings so they are on an even footing, then it keeps turning them, a little at a time, until each track sounds as different from the others as possible.

Where ICA shows up in real life

  • Pulling a voice out of background noise, for example in hearing aids or voice assistants.
  • Separating singing from music, like making a karaoke track.
  • Cleaning brain-wave recordings (EEG) by removing the signals made by eye blinks.
  • Separating overlapping signals picked up by sensors, or mixed together in images.
